Source code and README

Grid Edge Gateway

A site controller in Rust that sits between a grid operator and a commercial prosumer site. It speaks IEC 60870-5-104 to the operator's control centre and SunSpec Modbus to the inverters, chargers, heat pump and battery. A model-predictive planner schedules the site against real day-ahead prices; a real-time safety layer applies each country's rules: §14a EnWG in Germany, the ElWG feed-in cap in Austria, the 3% curtailment budget and flexibility contracts in Switzerland. What runs below is that code, compiled to WebAssembly.

What is simulated here

1

A delivery depot in Germany

One commercial site connected to the grid: 120 kWp of solar panels on the roof, four 22 kW chargers for its electric delivery vans (they come back in the late afternoon, charge overnight and leave in the morning), a 14 kW heat pump that heats the building and a 100 kWh battery.

2

A rule that lets the grid operator slow it down

On winter evenings everyone draws power at once and the neighbourhood transformer can overload. The German §14a EnWG lets the grid operator reduce chargers, heat pumps and batteries for a while, as long as a guaranteed minimum is left.

3

The gateway in between

The Grid Edge Gateway is the software between the grid operator and the depot. It receives the operator's commands (IEC 104), drives the devices (Modbus), keeps to the rules and, if you choose, plans the day by the electricity price. In the live demo you are the grid operator: press "Dim" and watch the site obey.

4

What happens when the reduction ends?

During the reduction the vans fall behind. When it ends, every depot in the neighbourhood catches up at the same moment, and the peak can end up higher than with no reduction at all. This is the rebound. The feeder calculator simulates many depots together to find the least harmful way to bring them back.

Feeder calculator: the best way to end a reduction

When a §14a reduction ends at the same time for many sites, their chargers and heat pumps catch up at once. The research behind this project (20 sites × 10 days × 33 cases, docs/study/rebound) found that a slow restart spreads the rebound over more minutes while its peak stays about as high, and that a site planning by price avoids most of it. Test any combination yourself: the page simulates every site with the gateway's own code and compares it with the same evening without a reduction.

Two highlighted days, and every day of 2025

The buttons pick the hardest days of the year. 20 January 2025 was a Dunkelflaute, with little wind or sun and a price of 583 €/MWh in the evening. On days like this, German grid operators are most likely to reduce heat pumps and chargers. 6 April 2025 had deeply negative prices at midday, the situation feed-in limits exist for.

Any other day of 2025 can be picked as well: it runs on that day's real prices and measured weather. The section 2025, evening by evening runs all 365 evenings for a feeder of 20 depots. This replays 2025 as it happened. Day-ahead prices are published one day in advance, so a whole year of them cannot be forecast, and the planner only needs the next 24 hours.

Towards the DSO

The gateway is an IEC 60870-5-104 controlled station, the protocol German DSOs require for telecontrol of plants from 100 kW. Commands are confirmed (or refused with a negative confirmation), measurements go out spontaneously with time tags, and the link runs over TLS with client certificates in the spirit of IEC 62351-3. The protocol stack is written from scratch and tested against lib60870.

Two layers of control

A planner (MPC) looks 24 hours ahead every 15 minutes and schedules battery, cars and heat pump against prices, the demand charge, departures, comfort and battery ageing. Underneath, a real-time safety layer runs every second and follows the plan only as far as the rules allow: the consumption floor, feed-in limits, the 6 A car minimum and departure deadlines never depend on the plan being right.

Towards the devices

Inverters and meter are found by walking the SunSpec model chain. Inverter limits are written without a revert timeout, so they stay in force if the gateway dies; chargers get a failsafe current of 6 A, which alone is below 4.2 kW. DSO commands are persisted, so a dimming survives a restart.

What the MPC planner adds, measured

Each plan is a convex quadratic program over 96 quarter-hours: battery dynamics with losses and an ageing cost per kWh cycled, a first-order thermal model of the building, each car's energy request and departure time (as ISO 15118 passes them to the charger), the DSO's announced dimming window as a constraint, day-ahead prices, and a demand charge on the highest quarter-hour of import. It is solved by Clarabel, an interior-point solver written in Rust. The same solver runs in the gateway, in the study and in this page. Forecast errors are handled where they matter: the battery keeps a reserve only in and just before an expected dimming, the one time a shortfall cannot be bought from the grid.

A Monte Carlo study measured it. The depot ran through 30 random-weather days each in spring and winter, on the real prices of those days, with the DSO dimming from 17:30 to 19:30. The same code ran 720 times.

Most of the value comes from prices, the peak and the building's thermal mass. Forecast errors matter much less, because the real-time layer absorbs them within seconds and §14a guarantees a floor. With the dimming moved to the afternoon, the chance-constrained reserve cost 5 € a day in spring and bought only a few seconds less above the floor. A plan that ignores the demand charge piles charging into the cheapest hours and raises the peak, and for a German commercial site that pays a Leistungspreis it can lose money.
